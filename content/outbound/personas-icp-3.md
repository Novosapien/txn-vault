# Buyer Personas: ICP 3, industry inference

> **Up:** [[buyer-personas]]
> **Parent ICP:** [[icp-definition]] Section 10, ICP 3. Archetype `Card-Adjacent Unaware, the Peer-Shadowed Operator`.
> **Dominant pain inherited by both:** **falling behind**, and they may not have registered it yet. A named peer in their vertical runs a card programme against a use case that transfers. They hold the direct customer relationship and they already move money to or from those customers. They have never considered a card.
> **Buying group as the ICP defines it:** CPO and CFO together, confirmed by Ian on 3 September. **The CTO is not in the buying group at this stage** and arrives only after the decision to have a card programme has been taken, which is after the sale this ICP is running.
> **Grounded on:** [[offer]] Section 2 for mechanism, Section 4 for what may be promised.

**Two personas, and two is the complete answer.** The ICP names two roles and excludes the third explicitly. Adding a technical persona here would be padding, and worse, it would aim messages at somebody who is not in the room.

**This is a different sale, not a different message.** There is a period of convincing on the value of a card programme at all before the conversation reaches which vendor. Both personas below are being sold the category first and TXN second, and every section reflects that.

**One naming clarification, because the two rules collide here.** [[offer]] forbids naming a competitor: that means a competitor of TXN's, another issuer processor. It does not forbid naming the prospect's own peer, which is a company in their vertical that launched a card programme. In this ICP the peer is both the qualification evidence and the opening of the message, so naming them is required rather than permitted. What may never be said is which processor that peer used.

**The prerequisite that does not exist yet.** ICP 3 cannot be operated without a maintained list of named peers per vertical. That list has not been built, and it is the largest outstanding piece of work on this ICP. Both personas below assume it exists; until it does, neither can be messaged.

## Persona 3.1: CPO, Head of Product or Head of Customer (Champion, co-leads)

**1.0 Archetype Name** The Peer-Shadowed Operator

**2.0 Role & Functional Identity**
- **2.1 Primary Function:** Owns the customer relationship and the product experience at a company that holds accounts and moves money, and has never thought of itself as being in payments.
- **2.2 Core Responsibilities:**
  - Owning engagement and retention across a customer base held as accounts rather than as transactions.
  - Running whatever the company has instead of a card: a stored balance, a wallet, store credit, a loyalty scheme, a payout flow.
  - Deciding where the next increment of customer value comes from, on a roadmap where every option is incremental.
  - Watching the competitive set, and being the person expected to have noticed when it moves.
  - Building the case for anything genuinely new, in an organisation with no history of doing this.
- **2.3 Scope, Seniority & Authority:** Scope: department to business. Seniority: Senior leadership to Executive. Decision Authority: owns the product roadmap and the customer proposition, does not hold budget for a new line, cannot commission this alone.

**3.0 Psychographic Profile**
- **Identity & Self-Image:** The person who understands the customer better than anyone else in the building, and who is quietly frustrated at how small the levers they hold actually are.
- **Core Values / What They Optimise For:** Depth of relationship. They are trying to own more of the customer's life, not more of the customer's clicks.
- **How They Measure Their Own Worth:** By retention and by whether customers do more with them this year than last.
- **Worldview & Biases Toward This Category:** Does not think of themselves as a payments person and is slightly intimidated by the label. Assumes a card is a banking product requiring a licence, a bank and a department they do not have, and has never checked whether that assumption is current.

**4.0 A Day in the Life**
Someone sent me a screenshot last week of a competitor's new card and my first reaction was that it was a gimmick, and my second reaction, about an hour later, was that it was not. We have the customers. We hold their balances. We already pay money out to them every month by bank transfer, which costs us something and takes two days and produces no relationship whatsoever. They have taken the same position we are in and made it into a product, and we did not think of it. Most of my week is the usual work: retention numbers that move a point at a time, a loyalty scheme that costs real money and generates a modest response, a roadmap of improvements rather than additions. When I raised the card thing internally the response was polite and immediate: are we allowed to do that, do we need a licence, is that not a bank thing. I did not have answers. I still do not, and that is the actual reason it has not moved.

**5.0 Pains & Fears (Expanded Dossier)**

**5.1 Operational Pains (Daily Fires)**
- **"We pay our customers by bank transfer and it is a cost with nothing attached to it."** Two days, a fee, no relationship, no visibility of what happens to the money afterwards.
- **"The loyalty scheme is expensive and the response to it is modest."** We are buying engagement with the weakest instrument available and we know it.
- **"Customers hold balances with us and cannot spend them anywhere."** The money is already ours to hold and we have given it nowhere to go.

**5.2 Strategic & Political Pains**
- **"A competitor built a line of business out of the position we are already in."** It is visible to our market. It may already be visible to our customers.
- **"Nobody here knows whether we are allowed to do this, so nobody advances it."** The blocker is not a decision, it is the absence of anyone who can answer the first question.
- **"Every idea I have is an improvement and none of them is an addition."** I am optimising a proposition rather than extending it, and I can feel the ceiling.

**5.3 The Deepest Fear:** That in two years the peer's card is a normal expectation in this vertical, the company is explaining why it does not have one, and the honest answer is that nobody could tell them whether it was possible so it never got a meeting. Not being beaten. Being late for an unglamorous reason.

**6.0 What They've Already Tried (and Why It Disappointed)**
- **Built or bought a loyalty and rewards programme.** It costs real money, delivers a modest lift, and does not touch the moment the customer actually spends.
- **Improved the payout experience: faster transfers, better notifications, a nicer screen.** Made a cost line slightly less annoying, changed nothing about the relationship.
- **Raised the card idea informally after seeing the peer.** It stalled on the first regulatory question because nobody in the building could answer it, and there was no obvious person to ask.
- **Looked at partnering with a bank.** The conversation was slow, the economics were poor, and the proposition on offer was somebody else's card with their logo on it.

**7.0 Decision Criteria & Objections**
- **What they weigh evaluating this category:**
  - Whether this is actually possible for a company like theirs, which is genuinely the first question and not a rhetorical one.
  - What it requires of them: a licence, a partner, a team, or none of those.
  - Whether it changes the customer relationship enough to be worth being first internally.
  - Whether they can run it with the people they have, because they will not be given a payments department.
- **Objections / sources of resistance:**
  - "Are we even allowed to do this? Do we need to be a bank?"
  - "We have never done anything like this and we have nobody who has."
  - "Our peer might be losing money on theirs. How would we know?"
  - "This is not on the roadmap and I do not have a budget line to put it in."

**8.0 Trigger Events (Why Now)**
- A named peer in the same vertical launching a card programme in the last twenty-four months, which is the qualifying event and the opening.
- A loyalty, rewards or membership programme going through a renewal or a review, which is when its cost is visible and its performance is being questioned.
- A payout or disbursement volume crossing a threshold that makes its cost a line somebody notices.
- Annual planning, which is the only moment a genuinely new line can be proposed.
- Funding or expansion that grows the customer base a card would serve, and with it the size of the prize.

**9.0 Information Diet & Trusted Voices**
- Their own vertical's trade press and events, where the peer's launch is being discussed and where this population actually pays attention.
- Peer product leaders in the same vertical, whose experience is trusted precisely because it is not a payments perspective.
- Vertical-specific communities and operator groups, rather than fintech ones, which they do not read.
- Their own customer research and support conversations, which is where the unspent balance complaint already exists.
- Reachable by a message that names the peer, describes what that peer built, and does not assume any payments knowledge. Fintech vocabulary loses this persona in the first line.

**10.0 Success Metrics (What "Good" Looks Like)**
- From payouts as a cost and a delay, to the payout being an instrument the customer keeps using.
- From balances sitting unspent, to balances that can be spent with controls attached to them.
- From buying engagement through a rewards scheme, to engagement attached to where the money actually goes.
- From a proposition they can only improve, to one they have genuinely extended.

**11.0 The Message That Lands**
Open with the peer, by name, and with what the peer built rather than with what TXN sells. This is the only ICP where a competitor's success is the opening, and the peer's programme is what makes the idea legitimate inside a company that has never considered it. Then answer the question that actually blocks this, which is whether they are allowed: TXN is the platform for any company launching and operating a card programme, and the buyer does not have to be a bank. Ian's own framing carries it, that it can be a trade platform, a marketplace, whatever. Then the promise that makes it survivable, which is that the programme can be run without employing card experts. Never name the processor the peer used. Do not open with who owns TXN. Use no payments vocabulary that a non-payments product lead would have to look up.

**12.0 Example Titles**
Chief Product Officer · Head of Product · Head of Customer Experience · Director of Loyalty and Membership · Head of Digital · General Manager, Consumer

## Persona 3.2: CFO or Commercial Director (Economic Buyer, co-leads)

**1.0 Archetype Name** The Underwriter of an Absent Line

**2.0 Role & Functional Identity**
- **2.1 Primary Function:** Holds the money at a company being asked to fund a capability it has operated successfully without, on evidence drawn from somebody else's business.
- **2.2 Core Responsibilities:**
  - Owning a P&L in which payments appear as a cost, never as a revenue line, and deciding whether that can change.
  - Carrying the cost of the current alternatives: transfer fees, disbursement operations, a loyalty scheme with a budget and a modest return.
  - Approving genuinely new lines, which at this company is rare and therefore consequential.
  - Judging whether a peer's visible activity constitutes evidence, or a story.
  - Answering for anything that adds regulatory surface to a business that has none today.
- **2.3 Scope, Seniority & Authority:** Scope: business. Seniority: Executive. Decision Authority: final budget signature, owns the risk appetite, and in this ICP holds the decisive vote because the case is financial before it is technical.

**3.0 Psychographic Profile**
- **Identity & Self-Image:** A steward. They are proud of a clean, comprehensible operation and are instinctively wary of anything that adds a category of risk the company has not carried before.
- **Core Values / What They Optimise For:** Return per unit of new complexity. They will fund growth; they resent funding overhead dressed as growth.
- **How They Measure Their Own Worth:** By margin and by the absence of surprises. A year with no unexpected costs is a good year.
- **Worldview & Biases Toward This Category:** Assumes anything touching payments brings regulation, capital requirements and audit obligations. Has never had reason to test that assumption, and it is the single largest barrier in this ICP.

**4.0 A Day in the Life**
The card idea reached me as a forwarded screenshot with a question mark, and I have not dismissed it, but I have not moved on it either. What I know is what it currently costs us to move money to customers, because that is a line I look at every month and it is not small. What I do not know is anything else: what a card programme costs to stand up, what it costs to run, whether it generates anything or simply moves a cost around, and above all what it does to our regulatory position, because right now we have almost none and that is a feature. I asked whether we would need a licence and got three different answers internally, all of them hedged. So the honest position is that I am not resisting this, I am unable to evaluate it, and in the absence of an evaluation the default is no. Meanwhile the loyalty budget comes up for renewal in the spring and I already know I am going to ask harder questions about it than last year.

**5.0 Pains & Fears (Expanded Dossier)**

**5.1 Operational Pains (Daily Fires)**
- **"Moving money to customers is a monthly cost and it produces nothing."** Fees, operational time, and no asset at the end of it.
- **"The loyalty spend is real and its return is arguable."** I fund it because stopping it feels risky, which is not a reason.
- **"Payments only ever appear on my P&L as a cost."** There is no line where it is anything else, and I have never questioned that.

**5.2 Strategic & Political Pains**
- **"I cannot evaluate this, so my default answer is no, and that may be the wrong answer."** Being unable to assess something is not the same as it being a bad idea, and I know the difference matters here.
- **"Nobody can tell me what it does to our regulatory position."** We are pleasantly unregulated. That is worth something and I do not know what I would be trading.
- **"The only evidence is a competitor doing it, and I have no idea whether it works for them."** I am being asked to infer a business case from a screenshot.

**5.3 The Deepest Fear:** That they approve it, and the company acquires a regulatory obligation, an audit surface and an operational function it did not have before, for a revenue line that turns out to be marginal. Not the money. Becoming a more complicated company in exchange for very little.

**6.0 What They've Already Tried (and Why It Disappointed)**
- **Negotiated down transfer and disbursement fees.** Delivered a real saving once, and left the underlying structure exactly as it was.
- **Reviewed the loyalty programme's return.** The analysis was inconclusive in a way that made continuing easier than stopping, which is the outcome they least respect.
- **Asked their bank whether a card was possible.** Got a slow conversation and a proposition with poor economics, and concluded, wrongly, that this was the only route.
- **Asked internally about the regulatory position.** Received three hedged and different answers, which is why the idea has not had a meeting.

**7.0 Decision Criteria & Objections**
- **What they weigh evaluating this category:**
  - What it does to the regulatory and audit position, which is the first question and the one that decides.
  - What it costs to stand up and to run, against the payout and loyalty costs already being carried.
  - Whether it needs a team, because a new permanent function changes the answer entirely.
  - Whether the peer's programme is actually working, and how that could be established.
- **Objections / sources of resistance:**
  - "What does this do to our regulatory obligations? We do not have any today and I would like to keep it that way."
  - "We have run without one for years. What has changed?"
  - "Who runs this? I am not funding a new department."
  - "I have no way to know whether it works for our competitor, so I have no evidence at all."

**8.0 Trigger Events (Why Now)**
- The loyalty or rewards budget reaching renewal, which is the moment its cost and its return are both on the table.
- Annual planning, which is when a new line can be proposed and outside which it cannot.
- Disbursement or transfer costs crossing a threshold that makes them visible at board level.
- A named peer's programme being discussed publicly enough that not having considered it becomes the question.
- Funding, an acquisition or expansion that increases the customer base and with it the size of the prize.

**9.0 Information Diet & Trusted Voices**
- Their auditors and their existing advisers, whose view on the regulatory question will effectively decide this.
- Finance peers in the same vertical, particularly anyone at a company that has already done it.
- Vertical trade press and industry bodies rather than fintech media, which they do not read.
- Their own cost lines, which are the most persuasive evidence available and are already in front of them.
- Reachable by a short message that leads with cost and with the regulatory answer, and that does not require them to understand payments to follow it.

**10.0 Success Metrics (What "Good" Looks Like)**
- From payments as a pure cost line, to a line with something on the other side of it.
- From loyalty spend with an arguable return, to engagement attached to actual spending behaviour.
- From disbursement as an operational overhead, to disbursement as a retained relationship.
- From a regulatory position nobody could explain, to obligations that are known, bounded and largely carried by the platform.

**11.0 The Message That Lands**
This buyer is not resisting, they are unable to evaluate, and the message has to close that gap rather than sell past it. Lead with the two costs they already carry and already look at: what it costs to pay customers out, and what the loyalty programme costs against what it returns. Then answer the regulatory question directly, because it is the actual blocker and no amount of product value gets past it: TXN carries scheme and regulatory obligations as a delivered outcome, surfacing what applies and applying it, and this is compliance confidence rather than compliance replacement, because TXN does not accept regulatory liability on their behalf. That boundary must be stated, not blurred. Close on the headcount point, that the programme is designed to be run without employing card experts, because their fear is a new department. Name the peer. Never name the peer's processor, never name a TXN competitor, and do not raise the owners at this stage.

**12.0 Example Titles**
Chief Financial Officer · Finance Director · Commercial Director · VP Finance · Head of Finance and Operations · Managing Director, Owner-Operator
