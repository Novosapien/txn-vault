# Buyer Personas: ICP 1, first program, card is essential

> **Up:** [[buyer-personas]]
> **Parent ICP:** [[icp-definition]] Section 10, ICP 1. Archetype `First-Program Card-Essential, the Committed Newcomer`.
> **Dominant pain inherited by all three:** **exposure.** They have committed to a product that does not work without a card, they have never issued one, and they have no incumbent to compare against, no current spend to model, and no way to sense-check a vendor's answer.
> **Buying group as the ICP defines it:** CPO leads, CTO validates feasibility and holds the technical veto, CFO funds it as part of the product build.
> **Grounded on:** [[offer]] Section 2 for mechanism, Section 3 for per-role pain impact, Section 4 for what may be promised.

**Why three and not two.** The ICP names three roles and each of them meets the exposure pain from a genuinely different direction: the CPO has promised something publicly, the CTO has to approve something nobody in the building understands, and the person holding the money is funding an unknown against a plan that has no comparable line in it. None of them can answer the other two's question.

**A note on the third persona.** ICP 1 has no employee band and sub-10 companies are in scope, so the economic buyer is frequently a founder or CEO rather than a CFO. The persona is written to that reality, and the titles at 12.0 carry both.

---

## Persona 1.1: CPO or Head of Product (Champion, leads the deal)

**1.0 Archetype Name** The Promised Launcher

**2.0 Role & Functional Identity**
- **2.1 Primary Function:** Owns the product proposition end to end at a company whose product does not function without a card, and owns the public commitment that it will exist.
- **2.2 Core Responsibilities:**
  - Defining what the card actually does for the customer: who holds it, what it can be spent on, what it is limited to, and how that differs from every other card in the customer's wallet.
  - Sequencing the launch so that the card, the account, the app and the onboarding all arrive together, because none of them is a product on its own.
  - Holding the roadmap against a founder and a board who read a card as a feature rather than as an infrastructure program.
  - Choosing the platform, in practice, because the decision is a product decision before it is a technical one.
  - Fronting the waitlist, the beta list and the early customers who were told a date.
- **2.3 Scope, Seniority & Authority:** Scope: business. Seniority: Executive or senior leadership, often a founding team member. Decision Authority: shapes and effectively selects the vendor, holds the roadmap, does not usually hold the final budget signature and cannot override the CTO's technical veto.

**3.0 Psychographic Profile**
- **Identity & Self-Image:** A builder who ships. They came here to make something people use, and they measure themselves against the gap between what they described in the deck and what a customer can actually do on a Tuesday.
- **Core Values / What They Optimise For:** Momentum, and a proposition that holds together. They would rather ship a narrower thing that works than a wide thing that waits.
- **How They Measure Their Own Worth:** By whether the launch date they gave is the launch date that happens, and by whether the product does what the pitch said it did.
- **Worldview & Biases Toward This Category:** Suspicious of infrastructure vendors as a class, because every one of them describes themselves the same way and none of the descriptions tells them what will actually be different in month four. Believes, correctly, that they are about to be sold something on the basis of a demo.

**4.0 A Day in the Life**
My morning is spent on things that are not the card, and my afternoon is spent discovering that all of them depend on it. I have a roadmap where the account opens, the balance funds, the app shows a transaction feed, and then there is a box that says "card" and behind that box there is a vendor I have not chosen and a process nobody here has run. I sat in a call yesterday where our engineer asked what happens when an authorisation is declined and who decides, and I realised I did not know whether that was our decision or the platform's, and neither did anyone else in the room. I have a waitlist with a number on it that goes up every week and a beta cohort I have already emailed twice with a date. Three vendors have been through here and all three said weeks not months, and I have no way to test that sentence. What I actually spend my energy on is not choosing a platform. It is trying to work out what I am not being told, from people who all use the same words.

**5.0 Pains & Fears (Expanded Dossier)**

**5.1 Operational Pains (Daily Fires)**
- **"I cannot get a straight answer on how long this takes, from anyone, including the people who want my money."** Every vendor gives a range and every range is anchored on a best case they will not describe. I have nothing to hold them to and no basis for the date I have already given.
- **"Every question I ask about the card turns into three questions I did not know existed."** I asked what happens when a card is used abroad and got an hour on scheme rules, interchange and authorisation routing. I do not know which of those are my problem.
- **"My engineers are estimating work in a domain none of them has ever worked in."** So the estimate is a guess wearing a number, and it is the number my roadmap runs on.

**5.2 Strategic & Political Pains**
- **"I have already told people a date, and I chose it before I understood what sat behind it."** The waitlist has it, the board has it, and one partner has it in an agreement.
- **"If I pick wrong, I will not find out for six months, and by then it is the whole product."** This is not a tool I can swap. It is underneath everything we sell.
- **"I am the one making this call and I am the least qualified person in the building to make it."** Product decides because it is a product decision, and product also has the least card knowledge of anyone who will touch it.

**5.3 The Deepest Fear:** That the launch slips twice, the second slip is caused by something they were told about and did not understand the significance of, and the company's credibility with its own early customers, its board and its team never fully recovers. Not that the product fails. That it is late for a reason they were warned about in a sentence they nodded through.

**6.0 What They've Already Tried (and Why It Disappointed)**
- **Read everything public.** Vendor documentation, developer portals, comparison pages, a competitor's engineering blog. It told them what is possible and nothing about what is normal, how long anything takes, or what goes wrong.
- **Ran a vendor bake-off on features.** Produced a matrix where every vendor ticks nearly every box, which resolved nothing, because the differences that matter are not features and did not appear on the matrix.
- **Asked a founder who has done it before.** Got one honest and alarming story about a specific vendor and a specific year, which is useful as a warning and useless as a decision.
- **Scoped building the pieces in-house.** Abandoned it within a fortnight once scheme certification and the ongoing mandate work surfaced, but it left them unsure whether that abandonment was correct or premature.

**7.0 Decision Criteria & Objections**
- **What they weigh evaluating this category:**
  - Whether the platform lets them change the product after launch without a re-integration, because the first version will be wrong.
  - Whether their own team can operate it, or whether they will need to hire someone who knows cards before they can run their own product.
  - How much of the scheme and regulatory obligation lands on them rather than on the vendor.
  - Whether the answers they are given survive contact with their CTO.
- **Objections / sources of resistance:**
  - "You have no clients, so every timeline you have given me is a projection."
  - "Everyone says weeks not quarters. Show me why yours is different, or stop saying it."
  - "I do not have the knowledge to evaluate whether what you just told me is true, and you know that."
  - "What happens when I want to change the product in month three and the answer is a change request?"

**8.0 Trigger Events (Why Now)**
- A launch date going into a board pack, a funding announcement or a partner agreement, which converts a plan into a commitment with a witness.
- The waitlist crossing a number that makes the silence embarrassing.
- The core banking or ledger platform going live, which makes the card the only unfinished piece and moves it from item eight to item one.
- The first payments or card hire starting, or failing to be filled after three months of trying.
- An EMI or payment institution authorisation progressing, which starts a regulatory clock against a decision nobody has made.

**9.0 Information Diet & Trusted Voices**
- Other founders and product leads who have already launched a card program, reached directly rather than through content. One honest conversation outweighs everything published.
- The accelerator or investor network they sit inside, which is where this population exists before it announces itself and where vendor recommendations actually travel.
- Fintech-specific newsletters and the operator communities around them, read for pattern rather than for vendors.
- Their own engineers' verdict on a developer portal, which they treat as a proxy for whether the company is serious.
- Reachable on LinkedIn, in their own words about what they are building, and by email that demonstrates the sender has read the product page rather than the company name.

**10.0 Success Metrics (What "Good" Looks Like)**
- From a date they defend nervously, to a date they state flatly, because they can describe what sits behind it.
- From a product with a hole where the card is, to a customer completing the whole journey in one session.
- From needing to hire card expertise before launching, to running the program with the team they already have.
- From a first version they are stuck with, to changing the card product in configuration after launch when the first version turns out to be wrong.

**11.0 The Message That Lands**
Lead on the thing they have publicly committed to and cannot yet defend: they have built the account and cannot yet build the card, and the missing piece is the one nobody in the building has done before. The claim that cuts through is the primary job, that they can run this program without employing card experts, because it answers their real fear, which is a permanent dependency on a hire they have not made. Say plainly that TXN targets under ten business days from signature to first live transaction, name it as a target rather than a result, and do not describe the process behind it. Do not compare TXN to any other vendor, and do not open with who owns TXN. This buyer has been given three identical pitches already, and the differentiator is being the only one who says which parts are proven and which are not.

**12.0 Example Titles**
Chief Product Officer · Head of Product · VP Product · Founder and CPO · Head of Card Products · Product Lead, Payments

## Persona 1.2: CTO or Head of Engineering (Approver, technical veto)

**1.0 Archetype Name** The Reluctant First Mover

**2.0 Role & Functional Identity**
- **2.1 Primary Function:** Owns the technical relationship with the platform, and is being asked to approve an integration in a regulated domain their team has never worked in.
- **2.2 Core Responsibilities:**
  - Evaluating the platform on its API surface, its failure behaviour and its operational model, not on its screenshots.
  - Owning the integration into the company's own product, ledger and internal systems, and owning it permanently.
  - Deciding what the company's authorisation logic is, and whether that logic lives with them or with the platform.
  - Being the escalation point for every processing problem for as long as the company exists, whoever caused it.
  - Holding the veto, and knowing that a veto exercised on instinct rather than evidence costs them credibility they will need later.
- **2.3 Scope, Seniority & Authority:** Scope: department to business. Seniority: Executive. Decision Authority: absolute technical veto, joint say on vendor, owns engineering hiring, does not own the commercial decision.

**3.0 Psychographic Profile**
- **Identity & Self-Image:** The person who keeps the promises other people make. They are pragmatic rather than purist, and they take quiet pride in having never shipped something that took the company down.
- **Core Values / What They Optimise For:** Predictability under failure. They care less about the happy path than about what the system does at three in the morning when something upstream is wrong.
- **How They Measure Their Own Worth:** By what does not happen. No incidents, no surprise dependencies, no architectural decision that has to be unwound eighteen months later.
- **Worldview & Biases Toward This Category:** Assumes every vendor's documentation describes the best case and omits the operational reality. Reads a developer portal as a character reference for the company behind it. Deeply suspicious of anything described as intelligent until told exactly where it sits in the request path.

**4.0 A Day in the Life**
I am being asked to sign off an architecture in a domain where I cannot yet tell a good answer from a plausible one, and that is a new experience for me. Normally I can read a vendor's docs and know within an hour whether these people have done this before. Here I read the docs and I learn what the words mean, which is not the same thing. This morning I spent forty minutes trying to establish whether an authorisation decision is ours or theirs, and got an answer that turned out to be both, depending on configuration, which is fine but is not what the first three paragraphs implied. My team is estimating a card integration none of them has done. I have written the word "unknown" four times in an architecture document I have to present on Thursday. And underneath all of it: whatever I choose, every processing problem this company ever has arrives on my desk, from an operations team that will not be able to fix it themselves and will not be wrong to escalate it.

**5.0 Pains & Fears (Expanded Dossier)**

**5.1 Operational Pains (Daily Fires)**
- **"I am evaluating a regulated system with no prior to compare it against."** I can assess an API. I cannot yet assess whether the operational model behind it is normal or negligent.
- **"Half of what I need to know is not in the documentation and the other half is in a specification I have to pay attention to schemes to read."** So I am dependent on the vendor's account of the vendor.
- **"Every question about failure behaviour gets answered with a feature."** I asked what happens when their side is unavailable mid-authorisation and got a paragraph about resilience.

**5.2 Strategic & Political Pains**
- **"I hold the veto and I have the least evidence I have ever had for using it."** If I block this, I have to say why, and my reason is that I do not know enough, which is not a reason anyone accepts.
- **"This decision outlives me in the role."** Whoever sits in my chair in three years inherits whatever I pick, and card platforms do not get swapped casually.
- **"Product is choosing and engineering is carrying."** The selection is happening on product criteria and the consequences are operational, and that asymmetry is going to matter later.

**5.3 The Deepest Fear:** A live incident, in production, on money, where the cause sits inside the platform, the fix is not theirs to make, and they have to stand in front of the founders and say they do not know when it will be resolved. Not being wrong. Being publicly powerless.

**6.0 What They've Already Tried (and Why It Disappointed)**
- **Read the API documentation of four vendors, cover to cover.** Learned the shape of the domain and nothing about which of them behaves well when something breaks, which is the only question they actually have.
- **Asked directly about failure modes, retries and stand-in behaviour.** Got marketing language back from two, a specification link from one, and a genuinely good answer from a person who then left the process.
- **Tried to hire someone who has done this before.** Three months, no fill at a price they can justify, and a growing awareness that this hire would be a single point of dependency even if they made it.
- **Sketched the pieces they could build themselves.** Concluded that the integration is tractable and the scheme certification and ongoing mandate work is not, which killed the idea and left the vendor decision exactly where it was.

**7.0 Decision Criteria & Objections**
- **What they weigh evaluating this category:**
  - Whether all functionality is genuinely reachable through the API, or whether the screens can do things the API cannot.
  - Whether behaviour is deterministic, and whether a sandbox call and a production call actually do the same thing.
  - Where the authorisation decision sits, and whether the company can take it themselves in real time when they want to.
  - What is logged, what is auditable, and whether anything non-deterministic sits anywhere near the transaction path.
- **Objections / sources of resistance:**
  - "Where exactly does the intelligence layer sit? If it is anywhere near authorisation, we are done."
  - "You are pre-launch. Nobody has ever run this in anger."
  - "Show me what happens when you are unavailable, not what happens when you are up."
  - "Which of these do you hold today, and which are ones you are building towards? Answer those separately."
  - "Who actually holds the PCI accreditation? You, or the people running your infrastructure?"

**8.0 Trigger Events (Why Now)**
- The core banking or ledger platform going live, which turns the card from a future problem into the only remaining unknown in the architecture.
- Being handed a launch date that was agreed without them, which converts an evaluation into a deadline.
- A failed or abandoned attempt to hire card or payments expertise, which forces the buy-versus-hire question into the open.
- A regulatory authorisation progressing, which introduces obligations they now own and did not choose.
- A security or architecture review scheduled by an investor or a partner, which makes every undecided dependency visible at once.

**9.0 Information Diet & Trusted Voices**
- The vendor's own developer portal and sandbox, used as evidence about the company rather than about the product. Gated sandboxes are read as a bad sign.
- Engineering peers at companies that already run a program, approached privately for the operational story rather than the architectural one.
- Scheme and regulatory primary sources, read reluctantly and only when a vendor's answer feels thin.
- Status pages and public incident histories, theirs and everyone else's.
- Reachable through technical substance rather than through outreach. A message that names a real architectural decision gets read; a message about transformation does not.

**10.0 Success Metrics (What "Good" Looks Like)**
- From an architecture document with unknowns in it, to one where every dependency is named and its failure behaviour is written down.
- From a sandbox that approximates production, to a sandbox they trust enough to test against.
- From being the escalation point for problems they cannot fix, to operations resolving their own configuration issues without an engineering ticket.
- From needing a card specialist on the team, to an integration their existing engineers own and understand.

**11.0 The Message That Lands**
Speak to them about mechanism, not outcome. The two facts that earn a reply are that everything is available through the API before it appears in any screen and behaviour is deterministic, and that the intelligence layer runs alongside the platform rather than inside the real-time transaction path, with a person in the decision loop. Both are structural claims they can test rather than promises they have to trust. Acknowledge the pre-launch position before they raise it, because they will, and because pretending otherwise ends the conversation. On accreditation, give them the real answer before they dig for it: TXN holds none of its own, PCI is held by Direct Transact, our co-founding owner, and the platform is operated within that accredited environment. Separate that cleanly from the standards the platform is engineered to. This buyer is the one who will check, and volunteering the distinction is worth more here than any capability claim. Do not lead with who owns TXN and do not compare TXN with any named competitor.

**12.0 Example Titles**
Chief Technology Officer · VP Engineering · Head of Engineering · Head of Platform · Technical Co-Founder · Director of Payments Engineering

## Persona 1.3: Founder, CEO or CFO (Economic Buyer, funds it)

**1.0 Archetype Name** The Funder of an Unpriced Line

**2.0 Role & Functional Identity**
- **2.1 Primary Function:** Holds the money and the runway, and is being asked to commit to a platform cost that has no comparable line anywhere in the company's history.
- **2.2 Core Responsibilities:**
  - Modelling what the card program costs to run at the volumes the plan assumes, and at the volumes the plan will actually deliver.
  - Deciding what gets funded this quarter against a runway that is finite and, at this stage, usually investor-set.
  - Answering to a board or an investor for a vendor commitment in a category none of them will interrogate well.
  - Judging whether the company is buying a platform or acquiring a permanent dependency.
  - At sub-30 headcount, doing all of the above while also being the founder who chose to build this product in the first place.
- **2.3 Scope, Seniority & Authority:** Scope: business. Seniority: Executive or Founder. Decision Authority: final commercial signature, budget owner, veto on cost and on term.

**3.0 Psychographic Profile**
- **Identity & Self-Image:** The person who keeps the company alive. They think in months of runway, and every commitment is measured against how many it costs.
- **Core Values / What They Optimise For:** Optionality and predictability, in that order. They will pay more for a commitment they can exit than less for one they cannot.
- **How They Measure Their Own Worth:** By whether the company can still choose. A decision that removes future choices is expensive whatever it costs.
- **Worldview & Biases Toward This Category:** Assumes infrastructure pricing is designed to be hard to model, and that the headline number is not the number. Has been surprised by a usage-based bill before, in another category, and has not forgotten it.

**4.0 A Day in the Life**
I am putting a number in a model for something I have never bought. Everything else on this sheet has a comparable: I know what engineers cost, I know what cloud costs, I know roughly what compliance costs because I have paid for it. This line I am building from a vendor's own description of their own pricing, which is not a source. Someone told me the fees step down as volume rises, which sounds good and is impossible to model without knowing the steps, and I have been told the steps are not discussed at this stage. I have a board meeting in three weeks where somebody will ask what the card program costs at ten times our plan, and my honest answer is a range so wide it is not an answer. Meanwhile my product lead is telling me the date matters more than the price, and my CTO is telling me not to rush, and both of them are right, and I am the one who has to choose which kind of wrong we are going to be.

**5.0 Pains & Fears (Expanded Dossier)**

**5.1 Operational Pains (Daily Fires)**
- **"I am modelling a cost line with no comparable and no numbers."** I have a structure described to me and no figures attached to it, so my model is a shape rather than a forecast.
- **"Nobody can tell me what this costs at scale, and scale is the whole point."** If the unit economics only work at a volume we have not reached, I need to know that now, not in year two.
- **"Every question about money gets answered with value."** I asked for pricing and was told about total cost of ownership. I know what that means.

**5.2 Strategic & Political Pains**
- **"This is a dependency, not a purchase, and dependencies are what kill optionality."** Once cardholders exist on someone's platform, leaving has a price I cannot yet see.
- **"I have to defend this to a board that will not interrogate it properly, which means I am the only check."** Nobody in that room will catch it if I get this wrong.
- **"The people telling me it is fine are the people who want it."** Product wants the date, engineering wants the clean integration, and neither of them signs.

**5.3 The Deepest Fear:** That eighteen months from now the company is locked into a platform whose cost grows faster than its revenue, and leaving means reissuing every card and rebuilding the integration, so the only real options are to absorb the cost or to raise money on worse terms because of it. Not overspending. Losing the ability to choose.

**6.0 What They've Already Tried (and Why It Disappointed)**
- **Asked three vendors for pricing.** Got a structure from each and figures from none, which is standard in the category and no less useless for it.
- **Built a model on assumed unit costs.** The output range was so wide that the model could not distinguish between vendors, which was the only thing it was built to do.
- **Asked an investor to introduce them to a portfolio company already running a program.** The introduction happened, the numbers did not, because the other company treats theirs as commercial in confidence.
- **Considered deferring the whole decision by a quarter.** Killed by the fact that the product does not exist without the card, so deferring the platform defers the company.

**7.0 Decision Criteria & Objections**
- **What they weigh evaluating this category:**
  - Whether the cost curve is predictable enough to model, and whether unit economics improve or worsen with growth.
  - The term, the minimums and the notice, because those set how expensive being wrong is.
  - Whether the platform removes headcount they would otherwise have to hire, since salaries are the largest avoidable cost in this decision.
  - Whether the vendor will still exist and still be servicing them in three years.
- **Objections / sources of resistance:**
  - "You will not give me numbers, so I cannot compare you to anything."
  - "You are pre-launch. What happens to us if you do not make it?"
  - "What is the real cost of leaving, in cards, in engineering time and in months?"
  - "How much of the saving you are describing depends on us not hiring someone, and how confident are you in that?"

**8.0 Trigger Events (Why Now)**
- A funding round closing, which converts a plan into a spending mandate with a clock on it.
- A board meeting where the launch date and its cost are both on the agenda.
- The runway model being rebuilt and the card program appearing as the largest unpriced line in it.
- The product lead committing publicly to a date, which removes deferral as an option.
- A failed senior hire in payments, which reframes the decision from cost to capability.

**9.0 Information Diet & Trusted Voices**
- Their investors and their investors' other founders, which is where real pricing intelligence in this category actually travels.
- Other founders and finance leads at the same stage, reached privately, for numbers that no vendor will state.
- Board members with operating experience in payments, whose opinion carries disproportionate weight for exactly one conversation.
- Very little published content. This persona does not read vendor material and will not be reached by it.
- Best reached through a warm introduction or a message that is short, specific and does not attempt to sell the technology.

**10.0 Success Metrics (What "Good" Looks Like)**
- From an unpriced line in the model, to a forecast they will defend in a board meeting without hedging.
- From unit costs that stay flat as the program grows, to a cost per active card that falls as volume rises.
- From needing to hire card expertise to operate the program, to running it with the team they have already funded.
- From a dependency with an unknown exit cost, to a commitment whose term, notice and exit they have read and accepted.

**11.0 The Message That Lands**
Do not open with the technology and do not open with pricing, because there are no numbers to give and a pricing conversation that produces no number damages trust. Open on the headcount question, which is the only large avoidable cost in this decision: the program is designed to be run without employing card experts, and that is the claim with money attached. Then the shape, in TXN's own words rather than a taxonomy: a fixed monthly licence fee plus volume-tiered fees that step down as monthly volume rises, so the cost per active card falls as the program grows. State plainly that figures come in a conversation rather than in a message. Do not raise the owners here, and never claim a total cost advantage over a named competitor.

**12.0 Example Titles**
Chief Financial Officer · Founder and CEO · Co-Founder · VP Finance · Head of Finance · Finance Director
