### CFO or Commercial Director (Economic Buyer, co-leads)

**1.0 Archetype Name** The Underwriter of an Absent Line

**2.0 Role & Functional Identity**
- **2.1 Primary Function:** Holds the money at a company asked to fund a capability it has operated successfully without, on evidence drawn from somebody else's business.
- **2.2 Core Responsibilities:**
  - Owning a P&L in which payments appear as a cost, never as a revenue line, and deciding whether that can change.
  - Carrying the cost of the current alternatives: transfer fees, disbursement operations, and a loyalty scheme with a budget and a modest return.
  - Approving new lines, which at this company is rare and therefore consequential.
  - Judging whether a peer's visible activity is evidence, or a story.
  - Answering for anything that adds regulatory obligations the company does not carry today.
- **2.3 Scope, Seniority & Authority:** Scope: business. Seniority: executive. Decision Authority: the final budget signature and the risk appetite; in this ICP holds the decisive vote, because the case is financial before it is technical.

**3.0 Psychographic Profile**
- **Identity & Self-Image:** A steward, proud of a clean, comprehensible operation, and instinctively wary of anything that adds a category of risk the company has not carried before.
- **Core Values / What They Optimise For:** Return per unit of new complexity. They will fund growth; they resent funding overhead dressed as growth.
- **How They Measure Their Own Worth:** By margin, and by the absence of surprises. A year with no unexpected costs is a good year.
- **Worldview & Biases Toward This Category:** Assumes a card program brings new regulation, capital requirements and audit obligations. In this ICP, that belief is the largest single barrier.

**4.0 A Day in the Life**
One of the cost lines they always look at is what it costs to move money to customers, and it is not small. The card idea has reached them from the product side, as a question rather than a plan, and they have neither dismissed it nor moved on it. Everything else about it is unknown: what a card program costs to stand up and to run, and whether it earns anything or only moves a cost around. When they asked what it would add to the company's regulatory obligations, they got several different answers internally, all of them hedged. They are not resisting the idea; they cannot evaluate it, and without an evaluation the default is no. Meanwhile the loyalty budget is coming up for renewal, and they already plan to ask harder questions of it than last year.

**5.0 Pains & Fears (Expanded Dossier)**
- **5.1 Operational Pains (Daily Fires):**
  - **"Moving money to customers is a standing cost, and it produces nothing."** Fees and operations time, and no asset at the end of it.
  - **"The loyalty spend is real, and its return is arguable."** I cannot say what we get back for it.
  - **"Payments only ever appear on my P&L as a cost."** There is no line where they are anything else, and I have never questioned that.
- **5.2 Strategic & Political Pains:**
  - **"I cannot evaluate this, so my default answer is no, and that may be the wrong answer."** Being unable to assess something is not the same as it being a bad idea, and I know the difference matters here.
  - **"Nobody can tell me what a card adds to our regulatory position."** The obligations we carry today are known, and that is worth something. I do not know what I would be trading it for.
  - **"The only evidence is a competitor doing it, and I have no idea whether it works for them."** I am being asked to infer a business case from another company's launch.
- **5.3 The Deepest Fear:** That they approve it, and the company takes on a regulatory obligation, an audit surface and an operational function it did not have before, for a revenue line that turns out to be marginal. Not the money: becoming a more complicated company in exchange for very little.

**6.0 What They've Already Tried (and Why It Disappointed)**
- **Negotiated down transfer and disbursement fees:** a real saving once, with the structure left exactly as it was.
- **Reviewed the loyalty program's return:** the analysis was inconclusive in a way that made continuing easier than stopping, which is the outcome they least respect.
- **Asked the company's bank whether a card was possible:** got a slow conversation and a proposition with poor economics, and concluded, wrongly, that this was the only route.

**7.0 Decision Criteria & Objections**
- **What they weigh evaluating this category:**
  - What it does to the regulatory and audit position, which is the question that decides.
  - What it costs to stand up and to run, against the payout and loyalty costs already carried.
  - Whether it needs a new permanent function, because a new department changes the answer entirely.
  - Whether the peer's program is working, and how anyone could establish that.
- **Objections / sources of resistance:**
  - "What does this add to our regulatory obligations? I want to know exactly what we would take on."
  - "We have run without one for years. What has changed?"
  - "Who runs this? I am not funding a new department."
  - "How is this priced at volumes I cannot predict, for a product we have never offered?"

**8.0 Trigger Events (Why Now)**
- The loyalty or rewards budget reaching renewal, when its cost and its return are both on the table.
- Payout or transfer costs crossing a threshold that makes them visible at board level.
- A named peer's program discussed publicly enough that not having considered it becomes the question.

**9.0 Information Diet & Trusted Voices**
- Their auditors and existing advisers, whose view on the regulatory question will in effect decide this.
- Finance peers in the same vertical, above all anyone at a company that has already done it.
- Industry bodies in their vertical.
- Their own cost lines, the most persuasive evidence available, and already in front of them.

**10.0 Success Metrics (What "Good" Looks Like)**
- From payments as a pure cost line, to a line with something on the other side of it.
- From a default "no" because nobody could evaluate the idea, to a decision taken on evidence.
- From a regulatory position nobody could explain, to obligations that are known, bounded and largely carried by the platform.
- From a card program that looks like a new department, to one run without employing card experts.

**11.0 The Message That Lands**
Make the case for a card program before the case for TXN: start from what card programs have done in their vertical, without naming a company, and set it against two costs they already carry, paying customers out and a loyalty budget with an arguable return. Then answer the regulatory question, because it decides: TXN surfaces the scheme and regulatory requirements that apply, in plain English, and applies them once the client confirms. This is compliance confidence, not compliance replacement, because TXN does not accept regulatory liability on the client's behalf.

**12.0 Example Titles**
Chief Financial Officer · Finance Director · Commercial Director · VP Finance · Head of Finance and Operations · Managing Director, Owner-Operator
