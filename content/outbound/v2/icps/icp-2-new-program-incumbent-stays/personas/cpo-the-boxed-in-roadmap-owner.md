### CPO or Head of Product (Champion, leads the deal)

**1.0 Archetype Name** The Boxed-In Roadmap Owner

**2.0 Role & Functional Identity**
- **2.1 Primary Function:** Owns the roadmap for the company's next card program, a new segment, a new region or a second card product, while the first program stays where it is.
- **2.2 Core Responsibilities:**
  - Defining the second program: who it serves, what the card does differently, and why it could not simply be a variant of the first.
  - Deciding whether the new program sits beside the first one or goes to a different platform, which is the whole decision.
  - Holding a roadmap where the constraint is external and the accountability is not.
  - Managing an organisation that is used to one processor and will feel a second as complexity before it feels it as capability.
  - Selecting the platform, because a new product or a new region is a product decision.
- **2.3 Scope, Seniority & Authority:** Scope: business. Seniority: executive. Decision Authority: owns the roadmap and effectively selects the vendor for the new program; does not hold the final commercial signature, and cannot force the technical decision.

**3.0 Psychographic Profile**
- **Identity & Self-Image:** An operator who has done this once and intends to do it better. They are past the phase of being impressed by infrastructure.
- **Core Values / What They Optimise For:** Freedom of movement. Their measure of a platform is how much of the roadmap it silently forbids.
- **How They Measure Their Own Worth:** Whether the product they described is the product that exists, and how much of the gap was their choice rather than someone else's limit.
- **Worldview & Biases Toward This Category:** Knows the category well enough to be unimpressed and to be specific. Learned on the first launch that what a platform can really do is found out after signature unless it is checked before, so they now ask about the second and third version of the product rather than the first.

**4.0 A Day in the Life**
They know exactly what the new program has to do: who it serves, where it launches, and what its card does that the first card does not. They have launched a card program before, and they carry what that launch taught them into this one. Their own team keeps asking why the new program cannot simply be a variant of the first. The honest answer is that the new customers need different things, and it takes a whole meeting to show why. Meanwhile the commercial team has a distribution deal with a card in it and a date on it. That date will not move because the card is not ready.

**5.0 Pains & Fears (Expanded Dossier)**
- **5.1 Operational Pains (Daily Fires):**
  - **"The new program needs things the first one never had to do."** A new market or a new segment brings its own controls, its own fee structure and its own BIN arrangement. None of them is the big thing, and any one of them can stop the launch.
  - **"The roadmap for the new program has to be ours."** The first version of a new product is never the last, and I cannot plan the second version on anyone else's queue.
  - **"Every month spent integrating is a month we are not shipping."** The new program needs engineering time, and so does everything else on my roadmap.
- **5.2 Strategic & Political Pains:**
  - **"Arguing for a second platform makes me look like I am adding complexity."** Internally it reads as my preference rather than as what the new program needs, and I keep explaining the difference.
  - **"I chose the first platform, and now I am the one asking for a second."** It can sound like an admission. It is not one: the new program has different needs.
  - **"The date came from the deal, and the accountability came to me."** The commercial team agreed it; product has to deliver it.
- **5.3 The Deepest Fear:** That the second program launches, the organisation experiences it as duplicated effort and split attention rather than as new capability, and the conclusion drawn is that the ambition was wrong. Not that it fails: that it succeeds and is remembered as an unnecessary complication.

**6.0 What They've Already Tried (and Why It Disappointed)**
- **Tried to run the new product as a variant of the first program:** the new customers needed different controls and a different card, and one program could not serve both groups well.
- **Built the new product's rules in their own systems, around the first program:** it worked for one case, did not generalise, and now needs maintaining.
- **Scoped the new product down to fit:** the narrower version tested badly with the people it was for, which moved a second program from a preference to a decision.

**7.0 Decision Criteria & Objections**
- **What they weigh evaluating this category:**
  - Whether what the new program needs is configuration or a build, and how they can check that answer before signing.
  - How much of the new program can change after launch without another vendor conversation.
  - Whether a new market is a configuration exercise or a second vendor relationship all over again.
  - Whether the organisation can absorb a second platform without it becoming a permanent cost.
- **Objections / sources of resistance:**
  - "We already have a processor and it works. Explain why this is not just complexity."
  - "You will tell me you support it. Every vendor does. How do I check before I sign?"
  - "Can I launch in several countries, and what does each one add in cost and engineering effort?"
  - "What happens to us when the thing we need next is not on your platform?"
  - "We can do nothing at all and be fine. Why now?"

**8.0 Trigger Events (Why Now)**
- A distribution or co-brand deal implying a card the first program does not issue, with a date attached and a counterparty watching.
- A new market landing page, regional domain or localised pricing page going live ahead of the card behind it.
- A funding round earmarked for expansion, which turns an ambition into a mandate.
- A second product line reaching the point where its card design has to be final.
- A licence or regulatory approval in a market the first program does not cover.

**9.0 Information Diet & Trusted Voices**
- Peers at companies running more than one program, sought out because that experience is rare and hard-won.
- Industry events and the corridor conversations at them, where this group compares notes honestly.
- Payments trade press and analyst commentary, read for market movement rather than for vendor selection.
- Their own commercial team's account of what distribution deals ask for, which is often where a new program's needs first surface.

**10.0 Success Metrics (What "Good" Looks Like)**
- From a launch date that depends on a vendor's queue, to a date the team sets and holds.
- From a new market being a vendor negotiation, to a new market being configuration and a BIN arrangement.
- From scoping the new product down to fit, to launching the version that tested well.

**11.0 The Message That Lands**
Open on the new market or product they have announced, and be specific about what that program needs to launch on its date, because this buyer knows the domain. The claim that cuts through is that program behaviour is configuration rather than bespoke code: each new program, market or product is set up with its own controls, BINs and routing, so the next one is a setting rather than another vendor relationship.

**12.0 Example Titles**
Chief Product Officer · Head of Product · VP Product, Payments · Head of Cards · Director of Product, New Markets · General Manager, New Segment
