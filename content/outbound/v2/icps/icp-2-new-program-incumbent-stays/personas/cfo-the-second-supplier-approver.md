### CFO or Finance Director (Economic Buyer, approves)

**1.0 Archetype Name** The Second-Supplier Approver

**2.0 Role & Functional Identity**
- **2.1 Primary Function:** Approves a second processing contract at a company that already pays for one, and owns the question of whether the combined position is defensible.
- **2.2 Core Responsibilities:**
  - Comparing the new arrangement against a price they already pay and already understand, which is the only benchmark in this decision and a very hard one.
  - Modelling the incremental cost of the second program against its incremental revenue, on a business case that is optional rather than necessary.
  - Owning the total vendor position: two contracts, two renewal cycles, two sets of minimums.
  - Judging whether the second program's economics stand alone or depend on volume moving across from the first.
  - Answering to a board that will ask, reasonably, why the company needs a second supplier.
- **2.3 Scope, Seniority & Authority:** Scope: business. Seniority: executive. Decision Authority: the final commercial signature; owns vendor consolidation policy; holds the veto that most often stops this deal.

**3.0 Psychographic Profile**
- **Identity & Self-Image:** The person who makes sure the company knows what it is spending and why. Not a blocker: a scorekeeper who insists the score is real.
- **Core Values / What They Optimise For:** Defensibility. Every number they present has to survive the question underneath it.
- **How They Measure Their Own Worth:** Whether the forecast holds. A commitment that behaves as modelled is a success even if it is expensive.
- **Worldview & Biases Toward This Category:** Understands processing economics well enough to know that headline rates are not the cost, and that the differences between vendors show up at volumes nobody quotes for.

**4.0 A Day in the Life**
They are being asked to approve a second supplier in a category where the company already has one, and they hold that request to a high burden of proof. Their first question is not about price: it is why the company needs a second supplier, and they have heard the answer and accept it. What they lack is a comparison. The only rate card they understand is the one the company already pays, and they are asked to judge a structure with no figures against a contract with a long record of invoices behind it. The business case is optional, because nothing breaks if the company does not do this. So they are funding upside, and upside is the first thing cut when the quarter is tight.

**5.0 Pains & Fears (Expanded Dossier)**
- **5.1 Operational Pains (Daily Fires):**
  - **"I am comparing an unpriced structure with an invoice I know line by line."** That is not a like-for-like comparison, and everyone in the conversation knows it.
  - **"The business case is optional, so it is the first thing that dies in a tight quarter."** I have to fund upside, and upside always loses to necessity.
  - **"Two contracts is two renewals, two minimums and two sets of commercial administration."** Small one by one, permanent together, and never in anybody's model.
- **5.2 Strategic & Political Pains:**
  - **"My board will ask why we need a second supplier."** I have to answer in terms of what the new program needs, in one sentence that does not sound like poor vendor management.
  - **"Adding a supplier is against how I run this function."** I spend my time consolidating, and I am being asked to do the opposite for a good reason, which does not make it comfortable.
  - **"If the second program underperforms, the cost line stays and the revenue line does not."** Minimums do not care whether the launch worked.
- **5.3 The Deepest Fear:** That they approve a second processing relationship, the new program grows more slowly than the plan assumed, and they spend the budget cycles that follow explaining a fixed cost against a revenue line that never arrived, in a decision that was visibly theirs. Not overspending: being personally attached to an optional commitment that did not pay.

**6.0 What They've Already Tried (and Why It Disappointed)**
- **Used the renewal of the existing contract to review the whole vendor position:** got a better rate on the existing book, and the new program still needed its own answer.
- **Modelled the second program on the first program's unit economics:** wrong, because the customer, the volumes and the ramp are different, and the model flattered the case.
- **Deferred the decision to see whether the commercial pressure was real:** it was, and the deferral cost negotiating time rather than saving anything.

**7.0 Decision Criteria & Objections**
- **What they weigh evaluating this category:**
  - Whether unit costs improve with volume, and at what volumes, because that decides whether the second program ever stands alone.
  - Term, minimums and notice, which set the cost of the decision being wrong.
  - Whether the arrangement removes headcount the second program would otherwise need.
  - Whether they can forecast the cost month by month before signing, rather than discovering it in arrears.
- **Objections / sources of resistance:**
  - "We already pay for this capability. Justify the second line."
  - "You will not give me numbers, and I am comparing you against an invoice."
  - "What does the second program cost over three years, including implementation and ongoing change requests?"
  - "What are the minimums, and what happens if the new program ramps more slowly than planned?"
  - "You are pre-launch. What is my position if you are not here in three years?"

**8.0 Trigger Events (Why Now)**
- The annual planning cycle, when the second program either gets a budget line or does not exist.
- A renewal date on the existing contract, which is the only moment the whole vendor position is genuinely open.
- A funding round earmarked for expansion, which changes the second program from optional to expected.
- A distribution or co-brand deal with committed volumes, which turns upside into a contractual number.
- A board question about why a signalled new market has not launched, which puts the cost of not deciding on the table.

**9.0 Information Diet & Trusted Voices**
- Their own invoices, above every other source. Real invoice data beats any vendor's model.
- Finance peers at comparable companies, for the one thing vendors will not provide: what other people actually pay.
- The board and its operators, whose view on vendor concentration shapes the policy this decision runs into.
- Almost no vendor content. This persona reads contracts, not collateral.

**10.0 Success Metrics (What "Good" Looks Like)**
- From a cost line they cannot forecast, to one they can predict month by month before it is committed.
- From unit economics that stay flat as volume grows, to a cost per active card that falls with volume.
- From a second program that only works if volume moves across, to one whose economics stand on their own.
- From an optional business case, to a signed commercial commitment with volumes attached to it.

**11.0 The Message That Lands**
This buyer's first question is why the company needs a second supplier at all, not the price. The claim that cuts through is that the program is designed to run without employing card experts, so a second program does not bring a second team to run it.

**12.0 Example Titles**
Chief Financial Officer · Finance Director · VP Finance · Head of Commercial Finance · Group Financial Controller · Director of Procurement and Vendor Management
