Objection Handling

How to answer the objections that come up in a TXN sales conversation

Version: v1.1 (revised from v1.0 review comments; v1.0 retained as the commented reference)

## 1. How to handle an objection

An objection is a request for confidence, not a rejection. Work it in four steps.

- Acknowledge. Take the concern seriously and repeat it back so the buyer knows they were heard.
- Understand. Ask a question before answering. What sits behind the objection is often not what was said.
- Reframe on value. Move the conversation from the narrow point to the outcome the buyer is trying to reach.
- Resolve with proof. Close with a specific, sourced proof point or a clear next step.

### The master frame: refuse the compromise

Most objections reframe onto one idea. Legacy processors force a choice between flexibility and reliability. First-wave modern platforms trade depth for ease. TXN gives control, speed and resilience in one platform across three surfaces (API, console and agentic AI), so a team does not have to choose.

### Never lead on price

Reframe on value and total cost of ownership first. Offer a narrower scope at the same rate before you move on price. If a discount is genuinely warranted, capture it on the Proposal tab of the deal pricing model so the revenue impact is visible and, in time, routed for approval by variance. Do not discount to win a conversation.

### Two rules that protect the message

- Attribute the 25-year heritage to Direct Transact, our co-founding owner, specifically. Never claim it for TXN the company, and never say built on, powered by, partner, parent group or bank-grade.
- Lead with what the client achieves, not the technology. Use the anchor verbatim: “Intelligence built in. Complexity taken out.”

## 2. Price and commercial objections

| What you hear | How to respond | Anchor / proof |
|---|---|---|
| “You are more expensive than X.” | Shift the comparison from licence price to the cost of running the program. With TXN the team runs it with far less manual effort: a self-serve console, pre-integrated services and an intelligence layer that remove routine work, so they need fewer specialists and less engineering time. Then compare like-for-like at their expected volumes. | Pillars 2 and 5 (time and effort saved); lower unit cost per active card. |
| “Your pricing is opaque.” | It is a published rate card with no hidden scheme, tokenisation or authentication mark-ups beyond the stated fees. The deal pricing model gives them a month-by-month cost forecast before they sign. | Deal pricing model; transparent rate card. |
| “We need a discount.” | Put a number on the value before you touch price (see 2.1). Then offer a narrower scope at the same rate. If you do adjust fees, enter them on the Proposal tab so the variance and approval are visible. Do not lead with price. | Section 2.1; Proposal tab. |
| “We cannot predict cost as we scale.” | Per-transaction rates step down as monthly volume rises, so unit economics improve as they grow. The model forecasts cost across the whole term. | Tiered pricing; pricing model forecast. |

### 2.1 Putting a number on the value

Value only persuades a CFO when it carries a number. Translate each lever into money in the client's own model.

- Faster time-to-launch. Weeks instead of quarters means card revenue and interchange captured months earlier. Multiply their expected monthly program revenue by the months saved.
- Fewer people. Running the program without card experts and with a smaller operations team avoids salary cost. Count the roles they will not need to hire.
- Lower unit cost at scale. Per-transaction rates step down as volume grows, so the cost per active card falls as they grow.
- No stitching or building. Avoid the integration and maintenance cost of wiring point solutions together or building in-house.
- No mandate treadmill. Visa and Mastercard issue scheme mandates periodically that every processor must implement. TXN absorbs that recurring engineering, the client does not.

Hold the fee side steady in the deal pricing model and let these savings sit on the client's side of the ledger.

## 4. Competitive objections

Never generalise. Name the competitor and answer on their specific weakness, honestly. TXN is pre-launch, so lead on the structural differences, not on a reference war you cannot yet win.

### 4.2 Point solutions, in-house build, and doing nothing

| What you hear | How to respond | Anchor / proof |
|---|---|---|
| “We will use a best-of-breed point solution for each piece.” | Consolidating into one platform means fewer vendors, lower TCO, clearer accountability and faster incident resolution. Ask who owns the problem at 2am when three vendors point at each other. | Single-platform consolidation. |
| “We will build it in-house.” | Build looks attractive at year one and painful at year three. Scheme compliance is not a one-off: Visa and Mastercard issue mandates periodically that every issuer processor must implement, and that engineering never stops. The three-surface model gives the control teams associate with building, without the maintenance cost or the mandate treadmill. Compare on time-to-market and five-year TCO. | Ongoing scheme mandates; five-year TCO. |
| “We will stay on our incumbent for now.” | The most common answer, and the most expensive. Anchor on their customers: the longer it takes to deliver a better card experience, the more likely their own customers look elsewhere. Every month on a legacy processor is throttled product velocity and accumulating integration debt, paid for in lost customer value. | Cost of inaction; customer churn risk. |

## 7. Ownership objections (joint venture)

These recur in most competitive procurements. Every answer must pass four tests: it must not imply TXN's technology is built on Direct Transact's system; it must not imply TXN outsourced its processing to a vendor; it must not read as an old South African banking platform; and it must return the focus to what TXN delivers.

| What you hear | How to respond | Anchor / proof |
|---|---|---|
| “Is this just Direct Transact, are you reselling it?” | TXN and Direct Transact are different products for different markets. What TXN draws from Direct Transact is not a technology stack, it is 25 years of proven processing reliability and the scheme certifications that come with it, because Direct Transact is a co-founding 50% owner. It is ownership, not outsourcing. | JV framework talking points. |
| “Isn't this an old SA banking platform repackaged?” | No. TXN is purpose-built for European fintechs, with its own architecture, API design, self-serve console and agentic AI. The reliability comes via ownership, as a foundation, not a dependency. | Purpose-built; co-founding owner. |
| “Who actually owns TXN?” | Answer directly, then return to value: TXN is a 50/50 joint venture, equally owned by Direct Transact and Paycorp. What that means for the buyer is backing by two established, regulated payments businesses, with a platform that is purpose-built and independent. | 50/50 JV; both co-founding owners. |

## 8. Quick reference

### Do

- Ask a question before answering. Reframe onto the outcome, then close with a sourced proof point.
- Name the competitor and answer on their specific weakness.
- Be honest about pre-launch gaps and lead with the mitigation.
- Attribute the 25 years to Direct Transact, our co-founding owner.

### Do not

- Lead with a discount, or discount to win a conversation.
- Claim the 25-year heritage for TXN, or say built on, powered by, partner, parent group or bank-grade.
- Promise a go-live date without naming the dependencies.
- Overclaim on references or AI. Lead with the outcome, not the technology.
